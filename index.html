02 — The basics

Understanding menstruation, plainly.

No jargon, no euphemisms — just the facts.

Menstrual phase — The uterine lining is shed, causing menstrual bleeding.
Follicular phase — Follicles develop in the ovary while the uterine lining begins rebuilding.
Ovulation — An egg is released from an ovary. The timing can vary.
Luteal phase — Hormonal changes prepare the body for a possible pregnancy.

A menstrual cycle is counted from the first day of one period to the first day of the next. Twenty-eight days is an average reference point, not a rule. Cycle length can vary naturally, and variation can be more common during the first few years after periods begin.

02A — The full process

What actually happens during a menstrual cycle?

01

Menstrual phase

If pregnancy has not occurred, hormone levels fall and the uterine lining leaves the body through the vagina as menstrual bleeding.

Days 1–5
02

Follicular phase

Follicle-stimulating hormone helps ovarian follicles develop. The uterine lining also begins building again.

Days 1–13
03

Ovulation

A rise in luteinizing hormone triggers the release of an egg. Ovulation does not necessarily happen on day 14 for everyone.

Around Day 14
04

Luteal phase

Progesterone rises after ovulation and helps maintain the uterine lining. If pregnancy does not occur, hormone levels fall again.

Days 15–28

Menstruation is a normal biological process. Hormones coordinate changes in the ovaries and uterus throughout the cycle. The uterine lining becomes thicker in preparation for a possible pregnancy. When pregnancy does not occur, hormone levels change and the lining is shed.

Period blood is not “dirty blood”. It is a mixture of blood, uterine tissue and other fluids. Its colour and consistency can naturally change during a period.

What can vary naturally?

  • Cycle length can differ between people and from month to month.
  • Bleeding amount can vary.
  • Cramps and other symptoms can differ.
  • Mood and energy may change around the cycle.
  • The first years after periods begin can be less predictable.
02B — Hygiene

Menstrual hygiene is about comfort, cleanliness and access.

01 — Hands

Wash your hands

Wash your hands before and after changing or inserting a menstrual product. This helps reduce the chance of transferring germs.

02 — Washing

Clean the external area

Water is enough for many people. If you use a cleanser, choose a mild, unscented product and use it externally. Avoid douching or putting soap inside the vagina.

03 — Products

Change products regularly

Change pads, tampons and other products according to your flow, comfort and the manufacturer's instructions. Do not keep a product in longer than its recommended maximum time.

04 — Disposal

Dispose responsibly

Wrap used disposable products and place them in an appropriate waste bin. Do not flush pads, tampons or wrappers down the toilet.

02C — Products

There is no single “correct” period product.

Choice depends on comfort, flow, cost, access, privacy, personal preference and individual circumstances.

02D — Health

Your period can tell you something about your health.

Consider talking to a healthcare professional if you notice:

  • Very heavy bleeding that repeatedly disrupts everyday life.
  • Severe or worsening period pain that makes normal activities difficult.
  • Bleeding between periods or unexpected bleeding.
  • A major or persistent change from your usual cycle pattern.
  • Periods stopping for a prolonged period when you are not expecting this.
  • Symptoms that make you concerned or significantly unwell.

Get urgent medical help if:

  • You feel faint, extremely weak or seriously unwell during heavy bleeding.
  • Severe symptoms are rapidly getting worse.
  • You have signs of a serious infection or another medical emergency.
Important: This section is for general education and does not replace advice from a qualified healthcare professional.
02E — After the bin

What happens to a pad after you throw it away?

Throwing something into a bin does not make it disappear. What happens next depends on the local waste-management system.

1

Used

Disposable pads contain absorbent materials and often plastic components designed to prevent leaks.

2

Wrapped & binned

The used pad should be wrapped and placed in a suitable waste bin — not flushed.

3

Waste facility

Depending on local infrastructure, waste may be transported to a landfill, treatment facility or incineration facility.

4

Environmental impact

Synthetic materials can remain in waste systems for a long time. Poor disposal can also contribute to litter, sanitation problems and blocked sewage systems.

Never flush menstrual products.

Pads, wrappers and other menstrual products can contribute to blocked pipes and sewage problems. Put disposable products in a waste bin and follow local waste-disposal rules.

02G — Quick reference

Everyday hygiene checklist

  • Wash hands before and after handling menstrual products.
  • Change products according to flow, comfort and product instructions.
  • Clean the external genital area gently.
  • Avoid douching and heavily fragranced products around the genital area.
  • Keep reusable products clean, dry and stored appropriately.
  • Wrap disposable products before putting them in the bin.
  • Never flush menstrual products.

Menstrual health is bigger than products.

Access to clean water, toilets, privacy, accurate information, pain support, affordable products and respectful treatment are all part of menstrual health.

03 — Perspectives

Biology meets culture.

Different communities have developed different ways of understanding menstruation.

EVIDENCE

Modern science

Menstruation is a hormone-regulated biological process involving the ovaries, uterus and reproductive hormones.

TRADITION

Ayurveda

Ayurvedic traditions contain historical ideas about menstruation and health. These traditions are diverse and should not be treated as one uniform set of beliefs.

CULTURAL IDEA

Moon connection

Menstruation has often been culturally associated with lunar cycles. Scientific evidence does not establish a simple universal moon-period connection.

BELIEF

Astrology

Astrology can provide cultural or personal meaning for some people, but astrological explanations of menstruation are not scientific explanations of the menstrual cycle.

06 — Access

A period should never become a barrier to participation.

Menstrual health also depends on affordability, sanitation, privacy and information.

What is period poverty?

Period poverty describes barriers that prevent people from accessing the products, facilities, information or support they need to manage menstruation safely and with dignity.

The problem is not only the price of a pad. Water, toilets, disposal systems, privacy, school or workplace facilities and accurate information all matter.

What access can involve

  • Affordable menstrual products
  • Clean and private toilets
  • Safe water and washing facilities
  • Appropriate disposal systems
  • Accurate menstrual-health education
  • Supportive schools, workplaces and communities

Public policy and community responses

Menstrual Hygiene initiatives in India

Government and public-health programmes have addressed menstrual hygiene through awareness, adolescent-health initiatives and access to products. Specific schemes and eligibility can change over time, so official government sources should be checked for current details.

Community organisations

NGOs and community groups have worked on menstrual education, reusable products, sanitation and access in different regions.

Why local data matters

National statistics can describe broad patterns, but they cannot automatically tell us what students on one campus believe or need. That is why this project includes its own survey.

09 — Words matter

Breaking the silence also means changing the language.

Respectful language can make health conversations easier.

MYTH
“She's dirty.”
Menstruation is a normal biological process. Menstrual blood is not inherently dirty or impure.
BETTER
“She's on her period.”
A simple, neutral statement removes unnecessary shame from a normal health experience.
MYTH
“Don't tell anyone.”
People should be able to ask questions and seek support without being mocked or embarrassed.
BETTER
“Let's talk about it.”
Open, respectful conversations make it easier to share accurate information and ask for help.

Language should be respectful and inclusive. Different people may use different terms for menstruation and their bodies.

12 — Questions

Frequently asked.

The questions people often hesitate to ask.

CYCLE Is a 28-day cycle compulsory? +

No. Twenty-eight days is an average reference point. Cycle length naturally varies between people and can change over time.

PRODUCTS Can menstrual cups be used by teenagers? +

Menstrual cups are not restricted to married people or older adults. Some teenagers use them successfully. Fit, comfort and instructions matter, so choosing an appropriate product is important.

DISPOSAL Can pads be flushed? +

No. Pads should be wrapped and disposed of in a suitable waste bin. Flushing menstrual products can contribute to blocked pipes and sewage problems.

HEALTH Is severe period pain always normal? +

Pain can occur during menstruation, but severe or worsening pain that interferes with everyday life deserves discussion with a healthcare professional.

CONVERSATION What if my family considers periods taboo? +

Start where it feels safe and practical. You can use neutral health information, speak with a trusted adult or healthcare professional, or simply begin with one question rather than trying to change everyone's views at once.

HEALTH Is menstrual hygiene only about pads? +

No. Menstrual hygiene includes safe products, clean water, sanitation, privacy, disposal, information and access to appropriate healthcare.

14 — Limitations

What this project can — and cannot — tell us.

Good research includes its limitations.

  • The survey reflects the people who chose to respond and should not automatically be treated as representative of the entire student population.
  • Self-reported answers can be affected by memory, interpretation and social comfort.
  • The project is designed for awareness and education rather than clinical diagnosis.
  • National or state-level statistics describe broader populations and should not automatically be interpreted as campus-specific data.
  • Long-term changes in knowledge or attitudes require continued engagement beyond a single project.
