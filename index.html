02 — The basics

Understanding menstruation, plainly.

No jargon, no euphemisms — just the facts.

Menstrual phase — Days 1–5. The uterine lining sheds.
Follicular phase — Days 1–13. An egg is prepared, overlapping with menstruation.
Ovulation — ~Day 14. An egg is released from the ovary.
Luteal phase — Days 15–28. The lining thickens for a possible pregnancy.

Menstruation is a normal, healthy biological process in which the uterine lining sheds through the vagina — typically once a month, from puberty (ages 10–15) until menopause (around 45–55). It's regulated by hormones across a cycle that usually lasts 21 to 35 days, with bleeding itself lasting 2 to 7 days.

Good hygiene practices

  • Change pads every 4–6 hours, tampons every 4–8, cups every 8–12
  • Wash hands before and after changing any product
  • Use clean water and mild, unscented soap externally
  • Wrap and bin products properly — never flush
  • Track your cycle to notice patterns early

03 — Many lenses, one process

Science isn't the only perspective worth knowing.

Four ways menstruation has been understood — across time and culture.

MODERN

Science & Biology

Hormonal shifts in estrogen and progesterone drive a 21–35 day cycle, triggering ovulation and the shedding of the uterine lining if pregnancy doesn't occur.

TRADITIONAL

Ayurveda

A menstruating woman is called Rajaswala — one in a state of rajas, the guna of movement and flow. Menstruation is framed as active release and renewal, not impurity.

FOLKLORE + DATA

The Moon Connection

The average cycle (~29 days) is close to the lunar cycle (29.5 days). Large studies find no strong overall link, but some long-term data shows intermittent alignment in individuals — a weak, real, but inconsistent effect.

CULTURAL BELIEF

Astrology

Some traditions link the cycle to lunar or zodiac phases, treating it as a time of heightened intuition. This is a belief system, not a scientific claim — presented here as cultural context.

05 — Period poverty

Access isn't equal — even now.

What's being done, and what's directly relevant to Maharashtra.

Period poverty isn't only about affordability — it's about access to products, clean facilities, and accurate information, all at once. Rural-urban gaps remain stark: in several states, hygienic product usage runs 20–30 points lower in rural areas than urban ones.

Progress is real, but gaps remain. Awareness is one of the few tools available to everyone, regardless of resources — which is exactly where this project comes in.

Suvidha (PMBJP) — biodegradable pads at ₹1 each, 8,700+ Janaushadhi Kendras nationwide.
Asmita Yojana (Maharashtra) — subsidised napkins at ₹5/pack via self-help groups, directly relevant to our campus community.
Menstrual Hygiene Policy 2024 — free pads & hygiene kits mandated in government schools nationwide.

07 — Beyond India

How other countries approach this.

Menstrual health policy is a global conversation — here's where a few places stand.

SCOTLAND

Free, by law

The Period Products (Free Provision) (Scotland) Act, passed 2020 and in force since August 2022, made Scotland the first country to legally guarantee free period products to anyone who needs them — in schools, colleges, and public buildings.

KENYA

Tax removed, pads provided

Kenya became the first country to eliminate tax on menstrual products, and has distributed free sanitary pads in public schools since 2017 — recognising that access, not just awareness, is what closes the gap.

INDIA

Progress in motion

India's approach has built gradually — from the 2011 Scheme for Promotion of Menstrual Hygiene, to Maharashtra's Asmita Yojana (2018), to the 2024 national policy mandating free pads in government schools. See our timeline below.

10 — Timeline: Menstrual Policy in India

How far the conversation has come.

2011

Scheme for Promotion of Menstrual Hygiene launched by the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, targeting awareness and access for adolescent girls aged 10–19.

2017

Government of India removes the 12% GST "tampon tax" on sanitary napkins, following sustained public campaigning.

2018

Maharashtra launches Asmita Yojana, distributing subsidised sanitary napkins to adolescent girls through self-help groups — the scheme most directly relevant to our campus community.

2024

Menstrual Hygiene Policy for School-Going Girls approved, mandating free pads and hygiene kits in government and aided schools nationwide.

2026

This project — an anonymous campus survey, an interactive myth-busting quiz, and a live awareness session — adds one small, local data point to that ongoing effort.

11 — Glossary

Quick definitions.

Rajaswala
Ayurvedic term for a menstruating woman — literally "one in a state of rajas," the guna of movement and flow.
PMS
Premenstrual syndrome — physical and emotional symptoms occurring in the days before a period, caused by hormonal shifts.
Period poverty
Inability to access affordable, hygienic menstrual products, safe facilities, or accurate information about menstruation.
Menarche
The first occurrence of menstruation in a person's life, typically between ages 10 and 15.
Menstrual cup
A reusable, bell-shaped device made of medical-grade silicone, inserted to collect (not absorb) menstrual flow.
Menopause
The natural end of menstrual cycles, usually occurring between ages 45 and 55.
12 — Frequently asked

Starter questions, answered.

Real anonymous submissions will be added here as they come in.

HYGIENEIs it normal for period length to vary month to month?+

Yes — variation in cycle length and period duration is common and usually not a cause for concern, though persistent large changes are worth mentioning to a doctor.

PRODUCTSAre menstrual cups safe to use overnight?+

Yes, most menstrual cups are safe for up to 8–12 hours of continuous wear, including overnight, when used and cleaned correctly.

STIGMAHow do I talk about periods with family members who see it as taboo?+

Starting with shared facts rather than direct confrontation often helps — this website's Learn and Perspectives sections are designed to be a low-pressure way to open that conversation.

13 — What we learned

In our own words.

Research & Content Lead

"The part that surprised me most wasn't a myth at all — it was learning that Ayurveda never framed menstruation as impurity in the first place. Researching Rajaswala completely changed how I understood where the stigma we grew up with actually comes from — it isn't ancient, it's a more recent cultural layer."

Survey & Data Analysis Lead

"I expected the myth scores to be uniformly high, since most respondents were young and in college. What the data actually showed was more specific — a handful of myths, like the ones around PMS and cycle length, were believed far more often than the rest. That pattern alone told us exactly where to focus our awareness session."

Website & Awareness Lead

"Building the myth quiz was easy compared to watching people actually use it. Seeing classmates pause on a question, second-guess themselves, then read the explanation — that's the moment this stopped feeling like a project and started feeling like it was actually doing something."

14 — Limitations

Being honest about scope.

  • Our survey sample is limited to students who chose to respond, which may not represent the full campus population.
  • Pre- and post-session comparisons reflect responses among attendees, not a controlled study — other factors may have influenced any change observed.
  • National statistics cited (NFHS-5 and related sources) describe India broadly and may not precisely reflect our local campus community.
  • This project reflects one awareness session; sustained behaviour change would require repeated, longer-term engagement.
